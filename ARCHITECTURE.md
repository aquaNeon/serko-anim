# Architecture

Notes on why this is built the way it is. The source is kept comment-free, so
the reasoning that would otherwise sit inline lives here.

## Layer stack

The globe is a fullscreen canvas plus a DOM overlay, mounted into one empty div
that Webflow owns.

```
z 40  DOM overlay   city pills, cards - text only
z 30  Webflow hero copy, eyebrow, buttons
z 20  <canvas>      dots, grid, rim, arcs, travel drop
z 10  Webflow gradient background
```

The canvas is transparent (`alpha: true`, clear alpha 0) and every layer discards
its back hemisphere in the fragment shader rather than hiding it behind an opaque
sphere, so the Webflow gradient shows through the globe.

The canvas is fullscreen rather than sized to the globe section: the globe art
bleeds wider than the viewport in the comp, and a single stable coordinate space
means there is no second layout to re-measure.

## What is canvas and what is DOM

Split by **drift tolerance**, not by convenience.

An arc endpoint has to *touch* its city dot — one pixel of drift is a visible
break. A pill floats 28px above the surface on a stem; one pixel there is
invisible. So anything pinned to the sphere is drawn in WebGL, and DOM carries
only text.

Pills must be DOM regardless: their width hugs the label (123px for
"San Francisco", 92px for "New York"), which needs real font metrics, real
accessibility, and editability in Webflow.

## The projection is the keystone

`GlobeCamera.project(lat, lng)` is the single source of truth. Dots, grid, rim,
arc endpoints, the travel drop and every DOM pill read from it. There is
deliberately no second way to turn a coordinate into a pixel.

The orthographic frustum is built **in pixels** — the camera is told where the
sphere centre should sit and how many pixels its radius should be, and derives
the frustum from that. Both the canvas and the DOM overlay then consume the same
camera, so there is no independent coordinate system that can drift.

Resize is applied inside the rAF tick, immediately before the render, and is
never debounced. If the renderer and the overlay updated on different ticks, arc
endpoints would visibly slide off their city dots during a window drag.

`tests/projection.test.mjs` pins this down: the sub-camera point must project to
the sphere centre exactly, a point 90 degrees out must land exactly on the limb,
screen distance must equal `R·sin(theta)` and depth must equal `cos(theta)` for
every test city, and none of it may change under relayout.

## Dots

One `THREE.Points` draw call for the whole field. Two visual tiers — small grey
everywhere, larger near-black on land — carried as a per-vertex attribute so both
still cost a single call.

The reference this was forked from
([community-globe-demo](https://community-globe-demo.webflow.io/)) built one
`THREE.Mesh` per dot: 3000 draw calls and over a million triangles for what reads
as a flat stipple.

### The lattice is uniform, on purpose

Dots sit on the same graticule the grid lines are drawn from, which is what makes
them read as following the lines. Every latitude ring gets the same number of
meridians, with no pole correction.

That does crowd dots towards the poles. On a pole-on view it would look wrong;
here the camera sits near the equator, so both poles fall at the extreme limb
where the projection compresses everything into the rim anyway. The crowding
lands exactly where the design already wants density.

An earlier version thinned meridians towards the poles in power-of-two steps. It
banded badly — `ceil` on the log meant density halved at the *first* ring off the
equator and stayed halved until 60 degrees, producing a bright equatorial stripe
and a hard seam where it stepped back. `tests/lattice.test.mjs` has a regression
test that fails if per-ring density stops being uniform.

### Dot size scales with globe radius

Point size is multiplied by `radiusPx / 500`, clamped to `[0.7, 1.6]`. Sizes were
chosen against the 1512px comp; without this the field visibly thins out on a
wider viewport.

## Land data

A 720x360 one-bit equirectangular raster, base64 in `src/data/landmask.js`,
generated by `tools/prebake-dots.mjs`. 5.2 KB gzipped.

Shipping a *mask* rather than a fixed list of dot positions means dot density
stays tunable at runtime without regenerating anything.

The reference demo fetched ~500KB of GeoJSON from raw.githubusercontent.com on
every page load and ran point-in-polygon over it on the main thread during hero
paint. Baking it removes the network dependency, removes the jank, and makes the
dot pattern deterministic — the arrangement signed off in design is the one that
ships.

Antarctica is clipped (`latMin -58`); it reads as noise at hero scale.

## Rim

An explicit 512-segment ring, not an emergent effect.

In the Figma art the globe's edge is an artefact of the dot lattice compressing
towards the limb, which means it breaks up wherever the compression happens to
leave a gap. The brief is that the edge is never empty, so it is drawn, with the
compressed dots sitting on top as texture.

Under an orthographic camera the limb is exactly a circle of radius 1 on the
plane perpendicular to the view axis, so a flat ring turned to face the camera
projects onto it precisely — no per-frame reprojection, and it stays correct if
rotation is ever enabled.

## Grid

Plain `LineSegments` hairlines.

The reference demo extruded every grid line as a `TubeGeometry` and rebuilt all
of that geometry on resize to hold a constant pixel width. That is the right
technique for a thick line, but this design's grid is a hairline — which is what a
one-pixel line draw gives for free on every driver, with no resize rebuild and a
fraction of the geometry.

The route arc is a different case: it is thick and gradient-filled, so it does
need real ribbon geometry.

## Rotation

Ships disabled, but the pipeline is fully 3D and the camera is driven by
`lookAtLatLng`. Turning rotation on later means animating those two numbers.

Faking a 2D projection would have been the same amount of code and would have had
to be torn out to add rotation, depth fade or occlusion.

## Configuration

No CMS collection. Places are data attributes on a Webflow component, so an
editor can swap New York for Chicago in the Designer without a redeploy:

```html
<div data-globe-place data-id="destination"
     data-lat="41.8781" data-lng="-87.6298" data-label="Chicago"></div>
```

Layout can also be overridden per-site with `data-radius-scale`, `data-center-y`,
`data-camera-lat` and `data-camera-lng` on the root element.

## Route arc

### Why a ribbon

`gl.lineWidth()` clamps to 1px on effectively every driver, so a 2px line has to
be built as geometry. Each of the 192 sampled points along the arc is duplicated
and pushed sideways, and the quads between them are filled — a ribbon of
triangles that reads as a line.

The sideways push happens in **screen space in the vertex shader**, scaled by the
viewport in pixels. The line is therefore exactly `uWidth` pixels wide wherever
it sits on the sphere and at any viewport size, with no geometry rebuilt on
resize. That is what welds the arc endpoint to its city dot.

### The lift profile

The arc rides above the surface on `r = 1 + lift · sin(π·t)`. Because `sin` is
zero at both ends, the endpoints land at radius exactly 1 — the same surface
point the DOM pin projects to — while the middle bows into a flight path.

### Draw-on, gradient and drop are one mechanism

Every vertex carries `aAlong`, its position 0..1 along the path. Everything else
is a comparison against it in the fragment shader:

- `uDrawProgress` — fragments past it are discarded, giving the draw-on
- `uHeadT` — where the travelling drop currently is
- saturation is `aAlong · (1 - step(uHeadT))`: a linear ramp from origin to
  destination, cut off ahead of the drop

That last line resolves what looked like two different briefs. The static spec
wants a gradient that is saturated at the destination; the animation wants dark
behind the drop and base blue ahead. They are the same expression — when the
drop reaches the end, the cutoff disappears and what remains is the static
gradient. The "mask a few pixels ahead of the drop" detail also falls out for
free, since ahead of `uHeadT` is already base blue.

The drop itself reuses the arc's geometry buffer with a second material. Its
width tapers along a short window behind `uHeadT`, rounded over the last 20% to
cap the head — a comet, following the arc's curvature for nothing.

### Occlusion

Exact for a sphere under an orthographic camera: a point is hidden when its
component along the view axis is negative *and* its perpendicular distance from
that axis is less than 1. Cheaper and more correct than a depth buffer, and it
keeps the canvas transparent.

## Pins

DOM, not canvas — pill width hugs its label, which needs real font metrics.

Measurements come from `design/hero-motion-spec.md`. The surface point is the
element's transform origin; the pill sits 31px above it, the stem spans 24px
with an 8px gap below the pill, and the dot carries its white ring and shadow as
`box-shadow`.

They update inside `stage.onFrame`, so they move in the same tick as the canvas.
Different ticks would show as the arc sliding off its dot during a resize.

## Flow

A small keyframe timeline in `src/flow.js`. Beats are data — `{track, at, dur,
from, to, ease}` — so retiming is editing `DEFAULT_BEATS`, not logic.

GSAP would have cost more than the globe for four tweens.

Pin beats use `outBack`, which overshoots past 1 deliberately to give the pill
its pop. `Pin.setAmount` therefore must **not** clamp: the raw value drives the
transforms and a clamped copy drives opacity. Clamping in the setter silently
throws the overshoot away and the pins land flat.

`prefers-reduced-motion` jumps straight to the end state rather than animating.
